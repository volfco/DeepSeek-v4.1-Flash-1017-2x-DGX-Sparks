## Proof of a committed point on the pair

The planner knows whether a write is pending. The store's in-memory index on each rank knows the exact point digest and all of its blocks. The experimental guard associates each live kept ID with its full point digest and cut at capture or successful restore. When the 500 ms rule proposes an early stale or drops its source, it tests the proposed victim with the normal compatibility-checked peer `has` operation. A no-forget abort closes the metadata transaction without reserving a destination or reading a block. On a missing proof, it tries an alternative with a present copy or defers to the 10 s patient decision. Each exceptional decision can probe up to eight candidate IDs, with each answer reused during that decision. A peer answer is synchronous and its measured delay is part of serving cost. This is a point-in-time presence proof. The normal restore still validates the data when it is actually read. ## Can the planner reuse a previous paired presence check?

The current wire protocol sends `keep`, `write_staged`, `write_cancelled` and `forget` from the head to the followers without a reply. A restore’s `commit` publishes its restored destination. Periodic tier-stat replies carry per-rank counts, including outstanding writes, and omit point identities. A follower may skip a keep or finish its write independently. [Wire operations and side implementations](../../../zig/patches/0016-dsv41-spill-blocks.patch)

The CPU fixture committed four nested turns with the same four-turn retention on both ranks. While the fifth keep was in flight, the oldest point still passed a true paired presence check. Finishing only the follower’s simulated device copy published its pending manifest and ran the store’s normal ancestor trimming. The oldest point then became absent on the follower and stayed present on the head. No later serving keep, explicit forget, restart or configuration change occurred after the positive check. A stored affirmative became stale even under a rule that clears every proof when sending a new keep. [Host experiment](committed-build/async-invalidation-test.zig) · [Result](committed-build/async-invalidation.log) · [Independent reproduction using `--case async-invalidation`](committed-build/async-invalidation-reproduction.log)

The existing periodic tier statistics also have a measurable blind spot. In a second two-rank CPU test, the head had committed its point and had zero local pending jobs. Its last peer sample still reported zero while a new stats reply correctly reported the follower’s pending write. Once both stores were genuinely idle, a second, normally refused follower keep produced the same zero/zero fresh job counts as the successful first keep. The first point existed on both ranks and the second only on the head. No blocks were read or destination slots reserved. A proof based on quiescence would need positive evidence for the exact point as well as an interval free from mutations. [Real stats path and points](committed-build/quiescence-test.zig) · [Host reproduction](committed-build/quiescence.log)

A broadly usable nonblocking guard needs a retention agreement or fresh key-specific information. One design would let a follower grant a short, bounded metadata lease for an exact compatibility epoch, partition, token extent and point digest, and keep the point protected against policy eviction until release or expiry. Existing periodic replies could carry lease grants after capability negotiation. At placement, the host would use only a still-valid grant and a matching local copy, falling back to the ordinary patient policy otherwise. The existing read pin takes a restore slot and schedules disk reads. A narrow alternative could use only successful ordinary restores within a positively observed all-rank quiescent epoch and invalidate globally on every possible mutation. Its coverage and its handling of pending writes and damaged shared blocks remain unverified. Neither design was deployed. [Store and tier implementation](../../../zig/patches/0016-dsv41-spill-blocks.patch)

### Bounded metadata lease on the real CPU store

An isolated prototype grants one exact digest, token extent, partition, rank and compatibility tuple per store. It checks a whole-point byte budget, allows up to one second per unrenewable grant, and gives the head half the negotiated time starting at its original send timestamp. The head checks a request nonce and the current store incarnation. Placement then needs the cached grant and a live local index check. Proof acquisition in the loopback harness used a test-only message outside placement. A deployed version would need capability negotiation, an independently known live incarnation, a bounded asynchronous acquisition path, and invalidation on a disconnect or failed restore. The test used shared host monotonic time. The two-machine implementation would also need a defined bound on relative clock drift. [Store patch](lease-build/retention.patch) · [Test-only loopback extension](lease-build/loopback-fixture.patch) · [Reproducible ReleaseSafe suite](lease-build/reproduce.py)

The fifth write was already queued when the follower granted temporary protection to the oldest of four nested histories. Publishing the fifth point ordinarily trimmed that oldest one. With a lease, the follower retained it until expiry. While the real loopback follower was busy for 80 ms, 1,000 cached decisions including the local store check took **111 µs total** on the development CPU. The pair stayed healthy. Neither rank reserved a restore destination and the check read no disk blocks. The next ordinary maintenance tick after expiry caught up the deferred turn trim. Reopening the same persistent store recovered the same key but generated a new incarnation, which invalidated the old view even when the new grant reused the same serial. A late response and a response for a different request nonce also failed. [Paired fixture](lease-build/loopback-test.zig) · [Loopback result](lease-build/checked01/loopback.log) · [Store results](lease-build/checked01/store-test.log)

The single-slot prototype increased the x86-64 ReleaseSafe store structure from 1,888 to 2,096 bytes, with a separate 144-byte view at the head. It charged 5,328 bytes against its disk budget for a leased point in the nested fixture. Since its successor already referenced the same blocks, the actual incremental retained data was 1,232 bytes, with zero extra written blocks and zero staging growth. Both stores held the same byte count again after expiry and maintenance. At a two-point disk cap, protecting the oldest point evicted a different independent point. At a 5,840-byte cap that could hold only one fixture point, a new keep failed with `no_space` until the 80 ms lease expired. Thus the lease can move restoration cost to another session or prevent a new backing write. [Pinned structure](lease-build/checked01/pinned-size.log) · [Candidate structure](lease-build/checked01/candidate-size.log) · [Pressure cases](lease-build/store-test.zig)

A true failed read of a deliberately damaged shared inode removed the leased point along with the two other affected manifests. Integrity failure and explicit forget take precedence over retention. The leader must invalidate cached views on authoritative failure events before it uses them again. The entire pinned store regression suite passed with leases disabled. These experiments establish a bounded host mechanism. They leave the coverage of timely grants, changes in successful backing writes, multi-rank invalidation, damage reporting and actual serving benefit open. ### Retention cost on the actual 300K private stores

After the five timed pressure windows, each rank contained 25 committed manifests in its synthetic tier. The census parsed only manifest metadata, checked block sizes and hardlink references against every committed point, and priced deletion of each target while holding the other manifests fixed.
